## Facts (with sources)
- In 1869, John Tyndall, at the Royal Institution in London, shone a bright light through a long glass tube (the surviving "blue sky apparatus" is 888 mm long) while gradually filling it with smoke. Seen from the side the beam looked blue; seen end-on the transmitted light looked orange/red. He had made a small sky and a small sunset in the lab. He thought dust/vapour particles caused it, but could not explain the intensity of the real sky's blue. (Royal Institution, "John Tyndall's blue sky apparatus", 1869; Ri basement laboratory.)
- In 1871, John William Strutt (1842-1919), then 28, published two papers on the colour and polarization of skylight. For particles much smaller than the wavelength, scattered intensity is proportional to 1/lambda^4. Blue light at 450 nm is scattered about 5.9 times more strongly than red light at 700 nm. (J. W. Strutt, "On the light from the sky, its polarization and colour", Philosophical Magazine 41 (1871) 107-120; HyperPhysics, "Blue Sky and Rayleigh Scattering".)
- Strutt became the 3rd Baron Rayleigh in 1873, on his father's death. His home laboratory was at Terling Place, Essex. He received the 1904 Nobel Prize in Physics for isolating argon. (Britannica, "Lord Rayleigh"; NobelPrize.org biography.)
- The blue of the daytime sky is sunlight scattered by nitrogen and oxygen molecules, not a pigment and not mostly dust. In 1899 Rayleigh showed the scattering law applied to individual molecules. Violet is scattered more strongly than blue, but sunlight contains less violet, ozone absorbs some, and human eyes are less sensitive to it, so the sky reads blue. (Wikipedia, "Rayleigh scattering"; NASA Space Place, "Why is the sky blue?"; Royal Observatory Greenwich.)
- At sunset the Sun's light crosses up to about 38 times more air than at the zenith; most of the blue is scattered out of the direct beam, leaving orange and red. (A. T. Young, "Air mass and refraction", Applied Optics 33 (1994) 1108-1110; NASA Space Place.)
- The Moon has no atmosphere, so its sky is black. Mars's thin atmosphere is full of fine dust; dust scattering (Mie) gives a butterscotch daytime sky, while dust forward-scattering makes the sky around the setting Sun blue. (NASA/JPL, "Sunset in Mars' Gale Crater", PIA19400, Curiosity, 2015.)
- Everyday analogy: a speed bump. A bicycle wheel feels every bump; a truck wheel rolls over them. A short blue wave "feels" the tiny air molecules; a long red wave mostly rolls past.

## Script (252 words)
In 1869, a scientist made a sunset in a glass tube, and almost explained why the sky is blue.

London, 1869. John Tyndall is scrubbing the dust out of the air in his basement laboratory.

He fills a glass tube with smoke and fires a beam of light through it.

From the side, the beam glows blue. From the end, it comes out orange.

Outside his window, the real sky is blue, and the air is clear.

One problem: the real sky has no smoke in it.

Luckily, the colours were right. Unluckily, the reason was wrong.

You see, smoke particles are big and lumpy. Air molecules are a thousand times smaller, and tiny obstacles scatter short waves far more than long ones.

Blue is scattered about six times more than red.

Think of a speed bump: a bicycle feels every bump; a truck rolls straight over them. Blue is the bicycle; red is the truck.

At noon, blue light bounces across the whole sky and into your eye from every direction.

At sunset, sunlight cuts through thirty-eight times more air, the blue is scattered away, and red comes through.

Two years later, a young Englishman named John Strutt — the future Lord Rayleigh — did the maths and showed the air itself could do it.

The rule is called Rayleigh scattering. It explains why the Moon's sky is black and why Mars's sky is butterscotch.

So the sky is blue because the air is stealing the blue from the sun.

The sunset is the receipt.
